Comparing Unitary and Federal States

Introduction

States organize public authority across their territory in different ways. Having several administrative levels, however, does not by itself identify a state’s constitutional form. This report compares unitary and federal states and examines where decentralization fits within that comparison. It draws on International IDEA’s comparative primer and provisions of Morocco’s Constitution, without assuming either model is superior in every setting.

I. Criteria for comparison

International IDEA presents federalism as combining self-government with shared government through a constitutional distribution of power protecting a sphere of constituent-unit autonomy.[C2] This directs attention to the source and protection of competences rather than simply the number of councils or administrative offices.

Three questions organize the comparison: who determines competences, how can their allocation be changed, and who resolves disputes about them? Within this framework, an elected local institution should not automatically be equated with a federal constituent unit. Equally, describing a state as unitary does not by itself specify how much authority is exercised locally. The rules organizing that authority still need examination.

Imagine two hypothetical states with the same number of territorial levels. Their constitutional protections, amendment arrangements, and mechanisms of participation could nevertheless differ. The comparison would then reveal different structures despite a similar institutional count. The example illustrates the limits of a single indicator; it does not classify an actual country without studying its constitution.

II. Implications and the Moroccan illustration

Article 1 of Morocco’s Constitution establishes decentralized territorial organization based on advanced regionalization. Articles 135–146 address regions and territorial collectivities, including free administration and competences.[C1] These provisions provide a basis for examining decentralization within Morocco’s constitutional framework. The term “regionalization” alone should not be treated as equivalent to federalism.

To answer a practical question, such as which body is responsible for a service, a researcher should trace the rules assigning responsibilities and resources. A general comparison table cannot replace that inquiry. Nor does a text assigning a competence establish how it is exercised in practice. This report presents no field data on regional performance and no statistical comparison of services.

IDEA’s primer discusses potential advantages of federalism alongside coordination difficulties and institutional complexity.[C2] These themes can inform evaluation criteria: clarity of responsibility, opportunities for participation, management of overlapping powers, and service delivery. Selecting one criterion while ignoring the others could bias the comparison before the evidence is considered.

The report therefore separates classification from evaluation. The first concerns constitutional structure. The second requires specifying what counts as good performance and identifying suitable data. Membership in a constitutional category cannot establish that every policy succeeds; an administrative difficulty likewise does not automatically change that category.

Conclusion

A careful comparison examines the source and protection of competences, amendment arrangements, and ways to resolve disputes. Decentralization and multiple local institutions must be interpreted within that structure; they do not independently prove federalism. In Morocco, the constitutional provisions on regionalization provide a starting point for studying territorial authority while keeping legal description distinct from empirical evaluation of results.
