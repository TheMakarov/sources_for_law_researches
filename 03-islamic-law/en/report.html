Custom and Habit in Islamic Jurisprudence

Introduction

Social practices raise a question beyond their description: does repeated use make a practice relevant to juristic reasoning? This report examines custom and habit through the conditions for recognizing custom and a limited teaching example. It uses the International Islamic Fiqh Academy’s resolution and a study published by the Academy of the Kingdom of Morocco. It does not attempt a complete comparison of schools or issue a ruling on an actual dispute.

I. From social practice to recognition

Resolution 47 describes custom through habitual speech, conduct, or omission and distinguishes recognized from unrecognized custom, as well as specific from general custom.[I1] The resulting analytical distinction is between establishing that a practice exists and establishing that it meets the conditions for recognition. Answering the first question does not settle the second.

This report uses “habit” in the general sense of repeated practice. It does not claim that all jurists agree on a single technical distinction between ʿāda and ʿurf. A fuller treatment of terminology would need to present particular authors’ definitions and identify their sources rather than attribute one definition to every school.

Consider a hypothetical trade practice concerning the packaging of goods. Before discussing its effect, the researcher should identify the trade, place, and period in question, then investigate its regularity or predominance. The example is constructed for analysis. It is not evidence that such a custom exists in any particular Moroccan profession or city.

II. Conditions and application

The resolution requires conformity with Sharia, continuity or predominance, existence when the transaction is formed, and no express contrary stipulation by the parties. It also calls for attention to changing customs.[I1] These requirements provide an order of inquiry rather than automatic approval of whatever frequently occurs.

In the packaging example, the researcher first examines the practice’s content, then its distribution and timing, and finally the agreement’s terms. If a valid express agreement specifies another packaging arrangement, its effect should be examined under the condition concerning contrary stipulations. That step must not be transformed into a claim that an agreement validates a practice contrary to Sharia. The conditions need to be considered together.

Al-Hussein Waggag’s study of Souss customs and human development provides a Moroccan discussion linking custom, juristic reasoning, and the Maliki context.[I2] This provides a connection with the Moroccan course context. It does not turn the Academy’s resolution into Moroccan legislation, nor replace specialist Maliki sources in a more advanced inquiry.

The evidence also has two distinct jobs. One source may establish the juristic conditions for recognizing custom; another kind of evidence is needed to establish that a specific practice actually exists. A student could explain the conditions accurately while applying them to an unproven description of social life. The report must therefore identify the limits of its evidence and label invented examples clearly.

Conclusion

Custom does not acquire juristic significance merely because it is widespread. Its content, scope, timing, and relationship with agreement require examination. This approach makes room for social practice while keeping recognition within stated constraints. The practical lesson is to separate defining custom from proving its existence, and both from determining its effect in a particular case.
